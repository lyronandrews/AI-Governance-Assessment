    A shared vocabulary

    Governance conversations stall when technology and L&D use the same words for different things. These are the terms that do the most work in the room — and the ones most often used loosely.

    AI management system
    The set of policies, roles, processes and records an organization uses to govern AI on purpose rather than by habit. It is the thing an auditor certifies — not the models, and not the tools.ISO/IEC 42001
    Risk assessment vs. impact assessment
    A risk assessment asks what could go wrong for the organization. An impact assessment asks what could go wrong for the people subject to the system — applicants, patients, customers, employees. Doing the first and calling it the second is the single most common governance shortcut.ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A.5 · NIST AI RMF MAP
    Risk tiering
    Sorting AI systems into levels so that controls are proportional. Without it, every use case gets either the full review or none, and in practice most get none.NIST AI RMF MAP 1 · EU AI Act
    AI actor
    Anyone whose decisions shape an AI system's behavior or consequences across its lifecycle — including the people who buy it, configure it, approve it, and use its output. Useful because it makes the accountability question bigger than the build team.NIST AI RMF
    Provenance and lineage
    Where data and models came from, under what permission, and what happened to them since. The question behind most AI legal exposure and almost every failed audit.ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A.7 · CSA AICM
    Evaluation (“eval”)
    A repeatable test of whether a system does what it is supposed to do on cases that matter, scored the same way each time. A demo is not an eval. A satisfied pilot user is not an eval.NIST AI RMF MEASURE
    Red teaming
    Deliberately trying to make a system behave badly before someone else does — adversarial prompts, misuse paths, and edge-case populations. Complements evals; does not replace them.NIST AI RMF MEASURE 2 · CSA
    Drift
    Behavior degrading over time because the world, the data, or an upstream model changed while your system stayed still. The reason a passing launch review expires.NIST AI RMF MANAGE 4
    Shadow AI
    AI in use without governance — personal accounts, unsanctioned tools, and AI features switched on inside software you already own. Almost always larger than the sanctioned footprint.
    Human oversight
    A named person with the authority, information, time and competence to override the system. Three of those four are usually missing, and competence is the one L&D owns.ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A.9 · EU AI Act Art. 14
    AI literacy
    A defined baseline of what everyone must understand about AI capability, limits, and acceptable use, plus deeper role-based requirements for those who build, buy, approve or oversee. Increasingly a legal obligation rather than a nice-to-have.ISO/IEC 42001 Cl. 7.2 · EU AI Act Art. 4
    Prompt injection
    Instructions hidden in content the system reads — a document, a web page, an email, a support ticket — that the model follows as though they came from you. The defining AI security problem, because the system cannot reliably tell data from instruction.OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications
    Agent permissions
    What an AI system is allowed to do on its own: which tools, which accounts, which spend, which irreversible actions. The security question shifts from what the model says to what it can reach, and over-permissioning is the fastest way to turn a bad output into an incident.
    Disclosure
    Telling people when AI is involved in an interaction or a decision that affects them. Distinct from explainability: one says that AI was used, the other says why it produced this result. Both are increasingly required, and organizations routinely confuse them.ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A.8 · EU AI Act
    System documentation (model card, system card)
    A maintained record of what a system is for, what it was tested on, where it fails, and what it must not be used for. Its value is in being current; a card written at launch and never revisited documents a system that no longer exists.
    Net value
    Benefit weighed against fully loaded cost and risk, rather than benefit against licence fees. Conventional ROI and total cost of ownership were built for software with predictable running costs; AI has variable inference costs, model changes you don't control, and a failure mode that can carry a legal bill.Gartner
    Return on employee
    The interim measure between launch and financial proof: adoption depth, time returned, experience. Legitimate as a leading indicator and dangerous as a destination. The discipline is converting it into an outcome someone in finance will sign, on a stated timeline.Gartner
    Baseline and counterfactual
    What the number was before, and what it would have been anyway. Without the first you cannot claim a change; without the second you cannot claim you caused it. Both are cheap before launch and close to impossible to reconstruct after.
    Workflow re-engineering
    Redesigning how the work is done rather than adding AI to the existing steps. The most common explanation for AI that demonstrates well and returns nothing: the tool is real, the saved time is real, and the process around it never changed, so nothing reaches the P&L.
    Assurance
    Evidence that controls work, produced by someone with no stake in the answer. Internal audit coverage, third-party attestation, or certification. Self-assessment — including this one — is not assurance.CSA STAR for AI · ISO/IEC 42001 Cl. 9

    The three frameworks, in one paragraph each

    ISO/IEC 42001 is a certifiable management-system standard. It tells you what to put in place — policy, roles, impact assessment, lifecycle controls, supplier requirements — and its Annex A is a usable checklist for a governance program. Reach for it when the question is how do we run this permanently.

    The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary and non-certifiable, organized around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. It is the best shared language for talking about risk across technical and non-technical teams, and its profiles let you adapt depth to context. Reach for it when the question is how do we reason about risk together.

    The Cloud Security Alliance contributes two things here. The AI Controls Matrix is a free, vendor-agnostic control set mapped to ISO 42001, NIST, and the EU AI Act, with an accompanying questionnaire for assessing vendors and a route to third-party attestation. The Trusted AI Safety Expert certificate, built with Northeastern University, is the workforce side: a defined body of knowledge for the people expected to do this work. Reach for these when the question is what specifically do we implement, how do we check a supplier, and who on our team is qualified.

    Where to go next

    The three frameworks above cover governance and control. They say comparatively little about scaling or about value, which is where most programs actually stall. These are the complements worth knowing by name:

    SEI and Accenture, AI Adoption Maturity Model
    Released June 2026 by Carnegie Mellon's Software Engineering Institute — the institution behind CMMI — with Accenture. Eight dimensions including workflow re-engineering and ecosystem, which most governance frameworks omit. Free from the SEI Digital Library, and the closest thing to an empirically validated maturity model in this space. Note that Accenture has a commercial interest in the finding that few organizations are scaling.Use it when: your gap is engineering and operating discipline rather than policy
    MIT CISR Enterprise AI Maturity Model
    Four stages, scored as a percentage, from experimentation through to future-ready. Its distinguishing claim is a link between maturity stage and financial performance, with the largest jump occurring between piloting and industrializing. Useful precisely because it gives you a research-backed answer to “why does this matter to the business.”Use it when: you need to make the board-level case for investing in the boring middle
    Gartner AI Maturity Model
    Five levels across seven areas — strategy, product, governance, engineering, data, operating model, culture. Built to produce a funded roadmap and a use-case backlog rather than a score, and it plugs into planning cycles that already speak Gartner.Use it when: your CIO already buys the research and you need the output to land in next quarter's plan
    The rest of the ISO/IEC 42000 family
    42001 is the certifiable management system, but it does not stand alone. 23894 covers AI risk management guidance, 42005 gives the method for the impact assessment 42001 requires, 5338 covers the AI system lifecycle, and 42006 sets the requirements for the bodies that certify you. If your organization is heading toward certification, 42005 is the one that does the most immediate work.
    Regulation as a de facto framework
    The EU AI Act, Colorado's AI Act, and sector rules increasingly set the floor regardless of whether you adopt a voluntary framework. The AI literacy obligation in Article 4 is the one that lands in L&D's lap, and it applies to organizations far outside the EU through their customers and supply chains.
    Threat-side references
    OWASP's Top 10 for LLM applications and MITRE ATLAS describe how AI systems actually get attacked. Neither is a maturity model, but they answer the question a security leader will ask in the room: what does the failure look like in practice?
    How to combine them without drowning

    Pick one for structure and borrow from the rest. A workable pairing: ISO 42001 for what to build, NIST AI RMF for shared language across functions, CSA's controls for what to check in a vendor, and SEI or MIT CISR for the scaling and value argument. Running two full assessments in parallel produces two roadmaps and no decisions.

    The bridge for L&D leaders

    Every one of these frameworks contains a competence requirement, and none of them tells you how to build it. That is the opening. Governance written by people who cannot explain it to the workforce does not survive contact with the workforce.
